Washington's evening paper ran a crossword on Sundays from February 17, 1924, on its
young people's page, and added a puzzle every weekday in November 1924. It is the longest
run of crosswords in the Library of Congress's free newspaper scans found so far: every
issue from 1924 to 1950 is there, so the whole run can be harvested without a subscription
database.

How much is established:

- **1924.** Alex DeJarnatt saved the Sunday puzzle pages for 41 dates from 1924-02-17 to
  1924-11-23 (no page found for 1924-06-29). The first, on February 17, is introduced as a
  new thing; February 24's says "another".
- **1925–1940.** The Newspaper Navigator dataset (see
  [research-notes-newspaper-navigator.md](../research-notes-newspaper-navigator.md)) has a
  crossword on 3,347 dates of the Star. By year it finds one on 61–86% of weekday issues in
  1925–30, 22–52% in 1931–36 and 82–89% in 1938–39.
- **The low years are the dataset's misses, not the paper's.** Of 30 weekdays in 1935 picked
  at random from those with no detection, 26 have the "Daily Cross-Word Puzzle" on one of
  three pages checked (the fourth, fifth and sixth from the end); the other four were not
  searched further.
- **1941–1950.** Detections nearly stop in 1941 (a handful a year, and a Sunday puzzle on
  1950-12-31). Whether the daily puzzle ended, moved or only changed its look has not been
  checked.

If the puzzle ran every day from late 1924 to 1940, that is about 5,000 weekday puzzles and
800 Sunday ones; the count is a projection from the checks above, not a tally.

Open questions: who made or sold the puzzles (no syndicate or copyright line was seen on
the crops checked, and the full pages were not read for one); whether the Sunday and daily
puzzles came from the same place; what happened in 1941; when the run ended. The Library's
scans of the Star go on past 1950.
